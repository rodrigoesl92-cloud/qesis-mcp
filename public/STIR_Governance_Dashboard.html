2026: the year infrastructure became the battlefield

The international scenario is chaotic in a precise, measurable way: states are desperate for resources they do not own. The technology confrontation between the United States and China has hardened into industrial policy on both sides; rare earths, water, carbon and energy are no longer background inputs but instruments of leverage. Governments under pressure drift toward authoritarian consolidation to keep their grip on those resources, and war itself has followed the resources into the server hall. In March 2026, Iranian drones deliberately struck Amazon data centers in the United Arab Emirates and Bahrain, the first time a state has targeted commercial data centers in wartime, and Tehran publicly threatened the region's largest AI compute sites in the weeks after (Georgia Tech analysis · CSIS · TechCrunch).

This dashboard does not report those events; it measures the terrain beneath them. What market desks and trade bodies register as quarterly disruption, the index registers as structural position: who is exposed, through which physical axis, and how fast the exposure compounds to 2080. The numbers were already showing the desperation before the drones flew. And they point to the only exit that is neither escalation nor surrender: infrastructure resilience is the one sustainable, dignified way out of the polycrisis.

What the data is saying: the executive read

  1. Legal sovereignty is not physical sovereignty. Belgium and Spain sit among the six most exposed states on Earth despite top-tier A-EU legal protection. Jurisdiction does not cool the physics: GDPR does not desalinate water or lay cable.
  2. Spain is the worst trajectory in the entire index: +11.6 by 2080 under BAU, driven by water stress 78.8 and rare-earth stress 91.5. Europe owns five of the six fastest-deteriorating states. The continent's problem is not where it stands; it is where it is heading.
  3. Scale is not sovereignty. The United States ranks #2 most exposed (65.5): cable exposure 100, critical-resource dependence 100. Hegemonic reach and physical fragility coexist.
  4. The sovereignty gate figure is withdrawn. This panel published “4 of 35 pass” with a named state list and a threshold to two decimals. No sovereignty_test field exists in the served index and only 32 states carry a composite, so the figure was not reproducible from the instrument that displayed it. Withdrawn 2026-08-12 under the D-104 disposition: publish the rule or withdraw the figure. It returns when the rule is served, not before.
  5. The trilemma is visible in the matrix. Water and electricity stress co-move (+0.41); rare-earth stress trades off against water (−0.26). Independence, efficiency, sustainability: the data lets states pick two.
  6. The gaps are findings. Taiwan carries no Extended score: BIG-gated, published as epistemic opacity rather than imputed away. An index honest about what it cannot see is the only kind worth trusting.

The trilemma lineage: pick two, always

Three generations of the same structural truth, one century apart. The political trilemma points up, the monetary trilemma points down; together they form a six-pointed constraint on every modern state. The Infrastructural Trilemma stands beside them: the physical layer both of the older ones silently rest on. Click any vertex to read what it stands for.

The Infrastructural Trilemma. Batista Silva (2026), the reaffirming truth
The lineageMundell and Fleming proved states cannot hold a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement and monetary autonomy at once. Rodrik generalized the logic to politics: hyperglobalization, national sovereignty and democracy do not fit in one state either. The Infrastructural Trilemma is the third generation, and the first one measured rather than argued. Across 35 states, no country simultaneously achieves algorithmic independence, hyperscale efficiency and ecological sustainability. The constraint did not soften as it moved from money to politics to infrastructure. It hardened into physics.
